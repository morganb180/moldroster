Mold testing vs a mold inspection: which do you need?

Reviewed by Mold Roster staff · Last updated November 1, 2026

Quick answer: A test only tells you that mold spores are present, and spores are always present. EPA, CDC and NIOSH all say routine sampling usually isn't needed, and there are no federal limits to compare results against. A licensed assessment looks for the moisture source and extent of growth and, in licensed states, sets the cleanup plan and the clearance check.

Should I buy a mold test kit?

Usually not. If you can see mold, EPA says sampling is usually unnecessary, and there are no EPA or federal limits for mold that a kit could measure against.

  • CDC does not recommend mold testing and says good sampling can be expensive.
  • NIOSH says short-term spore counts can't be interpreted in relation to health risks, and visual inspection or a musty smell is more reliable than air sampling.
  • No DIY kit identifies the moisture source, which is what has to be fixed.

What does a mold assessment include?

A licensed assessor inspects for visible growth, moisture and odor, may take samples, and, in Texas and New York, writes the plan the remediator must follow.

  • Texas: the assessor's protocol sets the quantities, locations, methods and clearance criteria (TDLR).
  • New York: the assessor's work analysis goes to you before remediation starts (Labor Law § 935).
  • After the work: the assessor checks the area and issues a clearance report (TDLR; Labor Law § 947).

When is sampling useful?

When mold is suspected but hidden, to check a cleanup, or when someone needs documentation. EPA says surface sampling can help show whether an area was cleaned well.

Sampling should be done by someone with experience, using recognised methods (EPA).

What does testing cost?

HomeAdvisor lists swab samples at about $200–$300 and air samples at about $250–$350, on top of an inspection that averages about $689.

See mold inspection cost.

Does Texas or New York require testing?

Neither requires sampling before every job. TDLR says sampling is not necessary in many cases. Both require a post-remediation assessment and clearance for licensed jobs.

See the remediation protocol.

Frequently asked questions

Are home mold test kits accurate?

Accuracy isn't the main problem: spores are always present indoors, and there are no federal limits to say what a result means. EPA, CDC and NIOSH don't recommend routine sampling.

Do I need testing before mold removal?

Not always. TDLR says sampling isn't necessary in many cases. A licensed assessor decides what's needed and sets clearance criteria.

Is air sampling worth it?

NIOSH says short-term air samples can't be interpreted in relation to health risks, and visual inspection is more reliable.
